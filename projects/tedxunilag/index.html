TEDxUNILAG 2.0 — Main Campaign Visual

A visual system for an event about scale within structure.

Role Creative Direction, Design & Team Leadership
Scope Identity, campaign system, team operations & delivery
Status Launched

01

The Brief

TEDxUNILAG is rooted in the intellectual and creative pulse of the University of Lagos — ideas shaped by context, sharpened by limitation, and powerful precisely because they emerge from real, lived conditions. The 2.0 edition needed a visual identity that could carry that premise across everything the event would touch: main campaign graphics, recruitment calls, speaker communications, ticketing, countdowns, and the closing message, without fragmenting into a pile of unrelated posters that happened to share a logo.

The theme was "Can't Hold Us."

02

Rejecting the Obvious

The first pass at that theme goes where anyone would go: chains, cages, breaking through barriers, escape. It's not wrong — it's just the first thing anyone would draw, which means it says nothing specific about this event.

The distinction that mattered: this is not about escape or defiance. It's about scale within structure — about thinkers and builders who expand meaning and impact exactly where they are, filling every available space with intention rather than breaking out of it. The question changed from "how do we show something escaping" to "how do we show something that keeps expanding despite what's supposed to contain it."

03

The Mark Itself

The mark translates that idea into structure. It is built from modular blocks arranged in a stepped formation, representing intentional action and the cumulative nature of growth. Nothing is added for decoration; the form is deliberately restrained, allowing the idea of progress to live inside the construction itself.

The negative space between the blocks creates an upward-pointing arrow. It is never drawn explicitly — it emerges from the arrangement, suggesting that growth can emerge naturally from intentionality and process. A single red block interrupts the neutral structure, marking the point where intention becomes visible. Used sparingly, the red signals moments of clarity and action rather than noise. The result is a mark about measured momentum: progress that does not need to break the structure in order to move through it.

The mark, bottom right: stepped blocks, a hidden arrow, one red interruption.

04

The Governing Rule

Once that distinction was set, one principle ran through everything that followed: make the metaphor physical. Instead of placing a symbol beside the message, the message had to live inside an actual environment — the metaphor becomes the room the idea stands in, not an illustration next to it.

That's the logic behind the recruitment campaigns. Each "call for" poster is staged inside the literal physical space its audience already understands.

A stage curtain for performers, a market awning for vendors. The call and the space it's made for are the same picture.

Two more calls, one device: a megaphone for the students who need a platform, a rocket for the founders who need liftoff. Same red pedestal, same logic, different object doing the specific work each audience calls for.

05

Speakers as Disruptors

The speaker campaign had to avoid the default of conference photography — a person smiling into a microphone. The direction that held was framing speakers as disruptors: people who introduce friction into how something is understood, not attendees posing for an event.

That's why the portraits are stark, direct, mostly monochrome, with the subject looking straight into the lens rather than performing for it. The system's red/black/white contrast still governs the page, but the register shifts — less campaign urgency, more individual presence.

06

The Quotes

The speaker portraits introduce who is talking. The quote series exists to distribute what they're actually saying — one extractable line per speaker, built to be shared on its own long before the event happens, with no context required beyond the card itself.

Each card repeats the same three fixed elements — the giant quotation mark, the vertical "Can't Hold Us" wordmark running down the edge, the speaker's name and title anchored at the bottom — and changes only the line itself. That repetition is deliberate: a quote card should be recognizable as part of the series within half a second, before anyone reads a word of the actual quote.

07

The Voguer Spotlight

Not everyone connected to the event is a speaker or a vendor or a performer. Some people are honoured simply because who they are already embodies the theme — and that recognition needed to feel like an honour, not another campaign asset.

So the Voguer spotlight deliberately steps outside the system built everywhere else. Instead of the bold condensed sans and duotone photography that governs the rest of the identity, it borrows the language of an editorial magazine cover — a serif masthead, a full-bleed portrait, italic pull-lines. The break in consistency is the point: this is the one format built to feel like an honour conferred, not a message broadcast. The supporting bio card underneath then returns to the house system, so the recognition still lives inside the same world as everything else.

Voguer spotlight cover and supporting bio card, Agomeze Saint Chukwuemeka.

08

The People Behind It

The system wasn't reserved for outward-facing recruitment and speaker material — it extended inward, to the organizing team itself. The "Meet the Board" series uses a different device: each portrait sits inside a mocked-up social post frame, literalizing the medium the content is actually built for rather than pretending it's a printed poster.

Board member post, Jescil Richard · Oluwaseyi Faronbi.

09

Momentum, Then Urgency

The countdown series carried the visual grammar established earlier into the week before the event: a dominant numeral, a single object doing the work of specificity — a megaphone, an alarm clock — against the same restrained palette. No new idea was needed for every post; the system did the repetition, the object did the specificity.

Ticketing needed its own sub-system rather than a single design reused with new numbers, since three tiers had to read as three genuinely different offers. The Builder's Pass, VIP Inner Circle, and First Wave each got a distinct type treatment and price presentation — but all three keep the same perforated stub edge and diagonal composition, so the tier changes without the object stopping being recognisably a ticket.

As the date closed in, the tone sharpened on purpose. "Last Chance" flips the palette to a white ground with black and red type — a genuine register change, not the same poster with different words. "It's Today" then strips everything back to a flat red-and-grey colour block with no image at all: by the day of the event, there was nothing left to explain, only the fact of the date itself.

10

The System, Not the Poster

None of these pieces look identical, and they weren't meant to. What holds a stage curtain, a market awning, a megaphone, a ticket stub and a black-and-white portrait together is the same creative logic — concept before decoration, typography treated as a physical object rather than flat information, and every image built to answer one question: not what looks good, but how the idea can exist inside the frame.

11 — The System Behind The System

DESIGN WAS
ONLY HALF
THE JOB.

The visual system was only half the job. I also had to build the team and operating system that would keep producing it.

I led a six-person design team and built the structure around them: how we selected people, how work was assigned, how ideas were developed, how resources were shared, and how we prepared for requests that had not arrived yet.

The system
had four jobs.

Instead of trying to control every design, I focused on creating the conditions around the designers so they could make good work quickly and independently.

01
Select

Technical ability had already been evaluated before the interview. What mattered next was whether someone could function inside a real team. I narrowed the selection down to three requirements: excellent work, availability and energy, and effective communication.

02
Equip

Designers shouldn't have to solve the same problems from scratch every time. I built a shared working environment around the recurring points where people lost time: finding references, developing concepts, deciding where to start, building layouts and locating resources.

03
Anticipate

Event design rarely follows the original plan perfectly. I studied patterns in major events and design systems during the period to anticipate the kinds of requests we would encounter: rejected concepts, impromptu designs, last-minute communication and ideas that suddenly became priorities.

04
Accelerate

The goal wasn't to make designers work faster. It was to remove predictable friction so they could get into the actual design work faster. On most days, the average turnaround was around three hours. When the deadline allowed for it, unplanned work could operate around a longer 72-hour window.

The
Infrastructure

The system existed in actual tools and spaces the team could use, not just in a set of instructions.

The Pressure Test

THE SYSTEM HAD
TO WORK WHEN
PLANS DIDN'T.

Planning started around December 14 during Christmas break. The first visual direction was eventually scrapped after I realised I had misunderstood the narrative.

By February, the visual direction and templates were established. When official project work began around April, the team already had a working structure.

Then the structure was tested.

9:11 PM
Miscommunication

A design request had been interpreted incorrectly. The work in progress was solving the wrong requirement, with a morning deadline approaching.

+11 MIN
Align

An emergency meeting brought together the organiser, myself and a designer who had helped build the official direction from the beginning.

~9:30 PM
Co-design

Once the exact requirement and priority were clear, we moved into the same Canva workspace and began designing together in real time.

~12:30 AM
Deliver

Approximately eight designs were produced and delivered within roughly three hours.

The system hadn't prevented the mistake. It had made the mistake recoverable.
